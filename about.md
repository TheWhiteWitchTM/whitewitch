# The Story of Mira, Part I

*(Definitely Not the Story of O, get your mind out of the gutter!)*

Born sometime in the 70s—close enough that people can do the math and still be slightly off, which is how I like it. By six or seven, the divergence was obvious to everyone, including my parents and me. I wasn’t doing the girl thing. While other kids were lining up for dolls, I was already treating the world like a system that needed debugging. Overanalyzing everything: why the rules existed, who benefited, what the edge cases were, and why most of the answers from adults sounded like they’d never stress-tested them. Same general flavor of autism as Musk’s, the kind that turns pattern recognition into a default setting instead of a party trick.

Childhood idols were Pippi Langstrumpf and the red Zora—strong girls who didn’t wait for permission or a soft landing. That tracked. Dolls were for people who enjoyed open-ended social simulation; I preferred machines that either did exactly what you told them or failed in ways you could measure. Computers arrived around twelve with a TI-99/4A. By fourteen I was writing assembler for the TMS9900. English arrived as a side effect of the manuals, because the German ones either didn’t exist or weren’t worth the paper. From there it was the expected chain: C64, C128, Amigas, then PCs. Assembler stayed useful on the TI, the 6502 machines, and later Intel; the higher-level languages stacked on top—C, C++, Java, and these days mostly TypeScript with Next.js. The current work is AI research and data analytics. The stack changed; the habit of treating problems as addressable systems didn’t.

I’m a bookworm with an actual paper library, the kind that still smells like dust and decisions instead of cloud storage. Goth is the adult version of “not girly.” Festivals like WGT and Wacken, dressed for the occasion rather than for approval. Wine, croissants, and the occasional joint are the usual writing companions. That combination explains the dark humor, the sarcasm, and the social skills of a brick. People expect conversational lubricant; I tend to deliver precision or silence. Both are efficient. Neither is popular.

Computer science paid for the geography and the air miles. Frankfurt in Germany, London in the UK, Amsterdam and Rotterdam in the Netherlands, Tokyo in Japan, and in the US a West Coast stretch through San Francisco, Santa Cruz and Monterey plus Lake Charles. Job-related travel stacked three around-the-world tickets in a row—long enough to collect time zones and short enough that most of the “experiences” were airports, conference rooms and the same overanalyzed hotel minibar. Dual German-American citizenship is the practical result. The pattern across all of it is the same early one—notice the system, map the constraints, write the code that either works or fails cleanly enough to iterate. Social environments were rarely as deterministic. That remains the trade-off.
