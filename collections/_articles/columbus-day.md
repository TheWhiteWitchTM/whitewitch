# 📜 Columbus Day: The Man Who Missed the Continent and Found a Genocide

Every October the United States takes a day to celebrate a man who never reached North America, never understood where he was, and left a paper trail of brutality that would get a modern contractor blacklisted.

## The people who actually showed up

Around the year 1000, Norse sailors from Greenland established a settlement at L’Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland. The site has been excavated. The evidence is physical: iron rivets, a spindle whorl, Norse-style buildings. They called the region Vinland. They were not “lost.” They were there.

They did not stay forever. They did not need to. The point is simple: people from Europe had already reached the North American continent centuries before any Spanish fleet left port.

## Where Columbus actually went

In 1492 Christopher Columbus landed in the Bahamas. On later voyages he reached other Caribbean islands and the northern coast of South America. He never set foot on the mainland of what is now the United States or Canada. He died believing he had reached the outskirts of Asia.

The continental landmass he is routinely credited with “discovering” was already inhabited, already visited by Norse sailors, and still completely unknown to him.

## What he wrote down

Columbus’s own logs and reports are not subtle. He describes the Taíno people of the Caribbean as suitable for servitude. He notes how easily they can be controlled. He requests permission to ship them to Spain as slaves. He records the taking of captives. Sexual violence against indigenous women is documented in the accounts of the period, including reports that reached the Spanish court.

Later governors and settlers under the system he helped establish continued the pattern: forced labor, mutilation as punishment, mass death from overwork, disease, and direct killing. The population collapse in the Caribbean in the decades after 1492 is not a matter of interpretation. Spanish colonial records and demographic estimates both show it.

None of this requires modern moral language. It is in the documents.

## The holiday

Columbus Day became a federal holiday in the United States in 1937. It was never a recognition that the man had reached North America. It was a political and cultural choice. The choice can be re-examined without pretending the primary sources say something other than what they say.

The Norse reached the continent. Columbus did not. The written record of what followed his arrival is ugly, repetitive, and contemporary. Argue with the documents, not the caption.
